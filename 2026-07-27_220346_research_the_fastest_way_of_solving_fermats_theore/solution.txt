Fermat's Last Theorem states that no three positive integers \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) can satisfy the equation \(a^n + b^n = c^n\) for any integer value of \(n > 2\). This theorem was conjectured by Pierre de Fermat in the 17th century, who claimed to have a proof that wouldn't fit in the margin where he wrote it. The proof remained elusive until 1994 when British mathematician Andrew Wiles presented a complete proof of Fermat's Last Theorem using advanced mathematical techniques developed during the 20th century.

The final proof by Wiles relies on deep mathematics involving elliptic curves and modular forms, rather than computational methods. While computations can be used to verify specific cases or to support research related to the theorem, no purely computational method has been devised that could prove Fermat's Last Theorem for all values of \(n\).

However, if you are interested in the computational verification of the theorem, it is worth noting that extensive computer searches have indeed verified the theorem for many specific exponents. These computations check for solutions to the equation \(a^n + b^n = c^n\) for various values of \(n > 2\) and ranges of integers, confirming that no counterexamples exist within those parameters.

In summary:
- Fermat's Last Theorem is proven by mathematical methods.
- Computational methods can verify it for specific cases but cannot serve as a proof for all cases.
